[E-LOW] CNN reported on November 16, citing seven U.S. officials, that new intelligence shows Iran drafting plans to target senior administration officials in Washington with a radiological dirty bomb or a chemical device. The United States has drawn up contingency plans, and officials also watch for chemical use against U.S. troops in the region. (S-001, S-002, S-003, S-005)

[E-MODERATE] The officials described the planning as giving Iran options, not as an imminent attack, and were divided on its credibility and on whether Iran has people in place. The White House said it is monitoring all potential threats and sees no credible threat to the homeland. Iran's Foreign Ministry called the report baseless. (S-004, S-006, S-010)

ANALYST ASSESSMENT: The report widens the Iran indicator without meeting the step to HIGH: intent against U.S. officials is long established, but no target is named publicly, no plot is charged, and the White House disputes the threat. A named official calling the intelligence credible, or announcing a related arrest, by November 21 is unlikely (HF-060-P1). A charge naming a target would change this.

7.3 Six Outages in Six Days, Each With an Internal Cause

[E-MODERATE] JPMorgan Chase blamed delayed transactions on November 16 on a software bug and found no evidence of a cybersecurity incident. United Airlines held departures for about 90 minutes the same day when its weight-and-balance system failed, and said there was no indication of a cybersecurity issue. (S-012, S-013, S-014)

[E-LOW] On November 17 an NSA spokesperson blamed an internal network outage on scheduled maintenance, and Apple blamed a services disruption on an internal update. None of the incidents has been confirmed as a cyberattack, and there is no evidence they are connected. (S-015, S-016, S-019)

Attribution rung reached: 1 (activity observed); each operator states an internal cause.

ANALYST ASSESSMENT: The cluster reads as a run of change-control failures across large networks, not a campaign: every operator named its own fault, and the two that addressed cyber ruled it out. An on-record finding by November 21 that any of the six was a cyberattack is very unlikely (HF-060-P2). An operator naming an intrusion or a shared vendor would change this.

7.4 The FBI Joins the FAA Router Review as the Suspect Function Is Switched Off

[E-MODERATE] The FAA found no indication of a cyberattack in the November 13 ground stop. Its preliminary review found that excessive data traffic destabilized older equipment and traced it to a radar facility in Amarillo and a radio site in El Dorado, where L3Harris was installing a router. Blocking both sites stabilized the network. (S-017)

[E-LOW] Secretary Duffy said on November 16 that the router is at an L3Harris lab in Florida with FAA staff and FBI agents he requested. The FAA disabled the suspect function on about 60 similar routers. L3Harris said it is supporting the investigation, and Duffy said officials have not determined whether user error, installer error, or a faulty router caused it. (S-020, S-021, S-022, S-023)

ANALYST ASSESSMENT: The FBI's presence is a check, not a finding; every official account still points to equipment and work practice. A public statement by November 28 that the lab found no malicious code or tampering is a roughly even chance (HF-060-P4). An FBI criminal or cyber case would change this.
