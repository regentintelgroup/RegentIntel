7.2 A Contractor Router, Not an Attack, Grounded Every U.S. Departure

[E-MODERATE] Transportation Secretary Duffy said on November 14 that a contractor was switching telecommunication lines from copper to fiber at a remote FAA site when a router began flooding the network. He said the router sent more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, cutting contact with aircraft and some radar feeds. The Secretary blamed L3Harris, which has run the FAA telecommunications network for more than 20 years. (S-001, S-002, S-004, S-035)

[E-LOW] Systems came back after the FAA isolated the work at the site. Duffy said officials are still determining whether L3Harris installed the router wrongly or the router was faulty; L3Harris did not answer requests for comment. A person familiar told Bloomberg on November 13 that the FBI does not believe the outage was a cyberattack or crime. An earlier Associated Press account said the fiber overhaul was not linked to the cause. (S-003, S-005, S-015, S-010)

Attribution rung reached: 1 (activity observed). Officials state that no malicious actor was involved; no actor is implicated.

7.3 Three Outages in Two Days Share No Cause on the Public Record

[E-MODERATE] Microsoft 365 services, including Outlook and Teams, failed worldwide from shortly before 1300 EST on November 12. Microsoft said the problem was introduced during an earlier network capacity upgrade, found no indication of malicious or cyber activity, and was still restoring service on the morning of November 13. (S-016, S-017)

[E-LOW] On November 13 the Weather Service posted that it had nationwide internet and communications problems, then said there was no radar outage and its radars were operating normally. A local Weather Service office described a regional problem with weather radios. (S-018, S-019)

ANALYST ASSESSMENT: The clustering is not evidence of a common cause: each operator named a change in its own network, and no official has linked the events. Public speculation about coordination ran ahead of the facts, and the Weather Service notice showed how fast a minor fault reads as a national outage during a crisis. DOT or the FAA announcing new rules for contractor work on the live air traffic network by December 31 is likely (HF-059-P6). A shared vendor or intrusion named by any of the three operators would change this.

7.4 Officials Suspect Iran in Commissary Freezer Failures; No One Says So on Record

[E-LOW] Two people familiar with the matter told Nextgov/FCW on October 29 that U.S. officials suspect Iran-linked hackers caused freezer failures at about six military commissaries on bases around the United States, beginning in late August. The outlet said the assessment could change. Reuters reported earlier that the Pentagon was treating the failures as possible cyberattacks. (S-022, S-023)

[E-MODERATE] On November 5, CISA, the FBI, and the Departments of Health and Human Services, Agriculture, and Transportation warned that attackers are targeting temperature controllers at grocery and cold-storage sites. The fact sheet said attackers could force units into heating or defrost cycles and block temperature readings; it named no actor. (S-020, S-021)

Attribution rung reached: 3 (threat actor association) as stated by unnamed officials; basis unstated. Evidence for rung 4 is not present in available OSINT.

ANALYST ASSESSMENT: If the commissary attribution holds, Iran has already reached equipment on U.S. military installations. The U.S. failures began before the French attack, so they were either early activity or unrelated. The record today is anonymous and caveated, so the rating holds. An on-record confirmation that the failures were cyberattacks, or an attribution to Iranian actors, by November 28 is unlikely (HF-059-P5). A joint advisory naming Shahid Kaveh or Iranian actors would change this.
